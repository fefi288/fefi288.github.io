COMPLETED COURSEWORK · STRUCTURAL DESIGN

Flanged Cross-Bar: Buckling Design & Simulation

For a team buckling-design assignment, I first developed an ABS cross-beam with a ratchet-style connector. Hand calculations showed that its predicted critical load fell below the required 150 N; its thin ratchet features also raised concerns about snapping under load. I redesigned the bar with flanges, a symmetrical cross-section, and a simpler slotted pin connection, then compared my hand calculations with SolidWorks buckling results.

These values are predicted buckling loads from analysis and simulation—not results from a physical destructive test.

150 NProject load target
179 NHand-calculated buckling load
178.09 NSolidWorks buckling prediction
36.6 gSimulated model mass

Design changes

Replaced the initial thin cross-beam and ratchet-style joint with a flanged, symmetrical cross-section and a slotted pin connection between printed segments. This improved the predicted buckling resistance, with tradeoffs including more material, greater design complexity and sharp corners that may concentrate stress.

Analysis outcome

The revised design exceeded the 150 N target by approximately 19% in hand calculations. The SolidWorks prediction of 178.09 N was within about 0.5% of the 179 N hand estimate. Calculated and modeled masses were 36.3 g and 36.6 g, respectively.

Compressed-Air Turbine: Shaft-Power Model

The next engineering milestone for my turbine-generator concept is to estimate the power available at the shaft. The starting point is a steady-flow control volume around the turbine, followed by a more realistic model of pressure loss and tank blowdown.

Ẇideal = ṁ cp Tin [1 − (Pout/Pin)(γ−1)/γ]

For an ideal gas undergoing adiabatic, isentropic expansion with negligible changes in kinetic and potential energy, this gives an ideal power limit. A first-pass shaft estimate is Ẇshaft ≈ ηt ηm Ẇideal, using turbine and mechanical efficiencies. It is not yet a measured or validated output.

Inputs still to be established

  • Absolute turbine inlet and outlet pressures
  • Inlet air temperature and mass-flow rate
  • Turbine and mechanical efficiencies
  • Nozzle losses and changing tank pressure

What comes next

Use nozzle and plenum geometry to estimate flow behavior, check available shaft power at representative conditions, and model the decline in power as the pressurized tanks discharge. Later, compare predictions with prototype measurements.

Theory only · No numerical power figure is published until the operating conditions and assumptions are defined.
